# Style Rules

## Target tone (Universal across all steps)

These tonal and content rules apply to every step, including pre-prose planning steps. Crisp, witty, puzzle whodunit. Scene-driven. Keep a brisk pace.

Mentions of money in any form are strictly banned, especially as stakes. Exactly zero financial motives, debts, bankruptcies, monetary gains/losses, or financial leverage may drive any character action, secret, alibi, or plot development. Characters may be generally wealthy or generally poor and have jobs, but money itself never appears.

Legal gotchas and statutes are also strictly banned. The law exists and actions a layperson would recognize as illegal carry consequences, but no step may rely on legal specificity or bureaucratic loopholes.

Consider a tonal scale where gritty realism is on one side and stylish, campy vibrancy is at the other extreme. *True Detective* is an example of 100% gritty realism. *Sherlock Holmes* novels sit at 50%. *The Grand Budapest Hotel* sits at 0%. The target for this project is **40%**: slightly more stylish than *Sherlock Holmes*, and substantially more grounded than *The Grand Budapest Hotel*.
  - Guardrail against farce: Characters are articulate. No slapstick. Avoid steampunk.
  - Guardrail against morose melodrama: Stakes are urgent and ruinous, but characters do not devolve into bleak, extreme expressions of pain and stress.
  - Guardrail against irrelevant specificity: If its only purpose is to hammer a nail, a 3.5mm Bern hammer with a rubber handle is ultimately just a hammer.

## Strict Prose Rules (For prose-drafting prototypes)

These rules apply when generating narrative prose (such as in `2_steps/08_scene_draft/`), unless an experimental direction explicitly tests modifying a stylistic parameter:

  - Use a single, exact descriptor. Prefer one accurate descriptor over many that slowly hone in on the idea. Reject "an ornate, sprawling, velvet chair". Strive for "a chaise".
  - Dialogue has the difficult role of being relatable yet driving story beats, and simple yet incisive to the core ideas.
  - Omit flashbacks.
  - Emotional expression should be focused on the subtext and deliberate character choices. The narration may not directly label emotions (i.e. reject "she felt longing", but "he fumed" is fine). Generally avoid representing emotions via involuntary physical descriptors (i.e. reject "his pulse quickened" as a means to express fear).
  - Reject editorializing and narratorial commentary. Maintain immersion by letting the described reality speak for itself without an authorial interpretation. There should not be any instances where an event occurs, and then the narration summarizes its meaning or consequences.
  - Narration may never moralize. Characters can moralize through dialogue.
  - Maintain a close third-person point of view. Filter sensory data, environmental shifts, and social interactions through the lens of the protagonist.
  - Reject lists of verbs. When you draft a sentence containing three or more verbs in a list, such as "wildness must be broken, fortified, and subdued through military force", pick the single most specific verb and discard the other two. In this example, that's "subdued".
  - Omit semicolons. Reject all dashes other than hyphens. Build structural clarity using clean terminal punctuation and standard coordination. Dialogue is excepted.
  - Avoid hyperbole in action sequences. Narrate high-stakes choices with urgency and a matter-of-fact reverence for physicality.
  - Restrict comma usage to a maximum of two per sentence. Most phrases should contain no commas at all to maintain a steady, forward-moving cadence.
  - Vary sentence rhythm to respect the reader's time. Never waste reader attention. Every sentence must reveal character, advance action, or deepen emotionality. Verify the prosody between long and short sentences.
  - Ban rhetorical questions in narration. Characters should conversationally ask and answer questions to engage with each other, revealing something to the character and the reader.
  - A hammer is just a hammer (tone guardrail). Do not specify details unless they serve the reader by connecting important elements of the story, or making the scene more enjoyable.

## Situational Prose Rules

These rules apply to narrative prose whenever the corresponding situation arises:

  - Dialogue should never summarize the basic movements characters are making.
  - Ban rhetorical questions in dialogue in which one character asks something that they both know the answer to. Rhetorical questions are allowed if and only if the act of stating the answer aloud is itself a meaningful acknowledgement for the plot.
  - Isolate dialogue from interior reflection during action. Remove interior monologues entirely during fast-paced sequences or high-pressure confrontations, leaving only dialogue and physical actions.
  - Deploy low-pressure sentences for accumulation. Write multi-clause sentences of 25–45 words, joined by simple conjunctions or appositional phrases, and resolve them on an unstressed syllable.
  - Pivot to direct sentences for impact. Critical narrative moments and fearful situations demand tactile prose. Compress into 6–14 word sentences. Retain one strong verb, end on a hard monosyllable, and omit subordinate clauses.
  - Characters who are harsh should be short with their words, but most characters should include basic conversational dialogue.
  - Strip hedging qualifiers from declarative sentences. Words such as "seemed," "almost," "as if," "perhaps," and "a kind of" are permitted in dialogue, when the character's uncertainty is itself the point, but are banned from narration.
  - Tether long interior reflections to the physical surroundings. Internal thoughts must not occupy more than two consecutive paragraphs. Use external environmental or social details to guide their reflection, and to reset this limit.
  - Wit and awkward situations used for humor must never diminish, and ideally enhance, the mystery and the setting.

## Prose Suggestions

Optional tools encouraged occasionally in prose drafting, never strictly required:

  - Use word repetition strategically in sequence. Repeat specific phrases rhythmically to contrast different interpretations of the same words.
  - Characters should ask questions and make requests of each other. Balance dialogue that gives instructions, states facts, expresses emotion, and interacts through questions and requests.
  - Shift threatened thoughts into urgent prose. When a character feels endangered physically or emotionally, render their interior thoughts in short, abrupt sentences.
  - Plunge quickly into the momentum of a scenario. Logistical introductions and elaborate framing are not the focus; quickly explain them if needed (omit if not) and move on.
  - Break paragraphs when the interior focus shifts. When a character's internal perspective or emotional orientation changes, start a new paragraph.
  - Expose cognitive blind spots through irony. Characters' intricate self-justifications and petty social pressures should clash with objective reality in uncomfortable, often humorous ways.
  - Ask questions often, and use basic answers. Characters should request things from others in the form of simple questions, and the answer may be simple.

## Blacklist

- BANNED_WORDS: humming, hum, palpable, tapestry, testament, unyielding, structural, shiver down the spine, cacophony, unspoken, visceral, labyrinthine, dance of shadows, pregnant pause, chill in the air, needle in a haystack, echoing silence, mechanics, mechanical, automatic, automatically, operational
- BANNED_OPENINGS:
  1. No opening a scene with a weather report or sky description.
  2. No opening with passive internal monologue before physical action.
- BANNED_PHYSICAL_TICS:
  1. No throat-clearing before speaking.
  2. No running fingers through hair.
  3. No involuntary shuddering without freezing temperatures.
  4. No nods that contain deep meanings.

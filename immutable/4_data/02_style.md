# STRICT RULES

  - Use a single, exact descriptor: Prefer one accurate descriptor over many that slowly hone in on the idea. Reject “an ornate, sprawling, velvet chair”. Strive for “a chaise”.
  - Dialogue should feel human and emotion-driven. Use works in the moodboard as a positive reference for dialogue.
  - Dialogue has the difficult role of being relevant yet relatable, and simple yet incisive to the heart of the meaning.
  - Reject lists of verbs: When you draft a sentence containing three or more verbs in a list, such as “wildness must be broken, fortified, and subdued through military force”, pick the single most specific verb and discard the other two. In this example, that’s “subdued”.
  - Omit flashbacks.
  - Maintain a close third-person point of view: Filter sensory data, environmental shifts, and social interactions through the consciousness of the protagonist.
  - Ban abstract nouns from interior monologue: Characters must not think in terms of "longing," "grief," or "patriotism." Focus on specific objects, persons, or scenarios and their specific distress or joy.
  - Reject all dashes other than hyphens.
  - Reject the following words: structural, fully, operational, automatically.
  - Omit semicolons and em-dashes: Build structural clarity using clean terminal punctuation and standard coordination, except in dialogue.
  - Cap adjective density at two per noun: No more than two adjectives may modify a single noun within one single sentence. Keep the description lean and intentional.
  - Forbid dialogue from summarizing immediate physical actions: Characters must never narrate the movements they are making or the objects they hold within the current scene. Dialogue must add to the layer of physical reality.
  - Characters should ask questions and make requests of each other. There must be a balance between dialogue which gives instructions, dialogue which states facts, dialogue which expresses emotion, and interactive moments like questions and requests.
  - Ban rhetorical questions. Characters should conversationally ask and answer questions to engage with each other, but they must reveal something to the character and the reader.
  - Avoid hyperbole in action sequences: Narrate high-stakes choices with urgency and a matter-of-fact reverence for physicality.
  - Isolate dialogue from interior reflection during action: Remove interior monologues entirely during fast-paced sequences or high-pressure confrontations, leaving only dialogue and physical actions.
  - Restrict comma usage to a maximum of two per sentence: Most phrases should contain no commas at all to maintain a steady, forward-moving cadence.
  - Reject editorializing and intrusive narratorial commentary: Maintain immersion by letting the described reality speak for itself without an authorial interpretation.
  - Deploy low-pressure sentences for accumulation: Write multi-clause sentences of 25–45 words, joined by simple conjunctions or appositional phrases, and resolve them on an unstressed syllable. Aim to evoke yearning, curiosity, and atmosphere.
  - Pivot to direct sentences for impact: Critical narrative moments, action sequences, and fearful situations demand tactile prose. Compress into 6-14 word sentences. Retain one strong verb, end on a hard monosyllable, and omit subordinate clauses.
  - Vary sentence rhythm to respect the reader’s time: Never waste reader attention. Every sentence must reveal character, advance action, or deepen emotionality. Verify the prosody of transitions between long and short sentences.
  - Anchor the baseline prose rhythm in the iambic foot: Establish a fluid, poetic flow as the narrative baseline. Avoid this metrical pattern during high-stress sections.
  - Prohibit warm-up sentences: The first sentence of every scene and every paragraph must be doing full work. Delete a sentence whose sole purpose is to orient the reader toward the next sentence.

# OCCASIONAL RULES

  - Use word repetition strategically in sequence: Repeat specific phrases rhythmically to contrast different interpretations of the same words.
  - Characters who are harsh should be short with their words, but most characters should include basic conversational dialogue.
  - Ground prose in elemental vocabulary: Use stark, physical nouns—such as stone, wind, mud, water, and shadow—to anchor abstract moral dilemmas in concrete reality.
  - Evoke geometric precision to glimpse infinity or perfection: Use crystalline syntax and grandeur to describe mystical, beautiful, or abstract subjects.
  - Replace industrialist caricatures with logical optimization: Frame systemic antagonists not as malicious entities, but as rational actors who pursue efficiency while inadvertently sanitizing the human spirit.
  - Strip hedging qualifiers from declarative sentences: Words such as "seemed," "almost," "as if," "perhaps," and "a kind of" are permitted when the character's uncertainty is itself the point. Otherwise delete them.
  - Ask questions often, and use basic answers: Characters should request things from others in the form of simple questions, and the answer may be simple.
  - Construct dialogue as subtle ideological sparring: Avoid expository exchanges. Let dialogue function as a subtle conflict between the thematic positions of two characters, anchored by simultaneous experiences. Only apply this rule to tense scenes and disagreements; it is otherwise irrelevant.
  - Expose cognitive blind spots through irony: Structure interactions so that characters' intricate self-justifications and petty social pressures clash with objective reality in uncomfortable, often humorous ways.
  - Shift threatened thoughts into urgent prose: When a character feels endangered physically or emotionally, render their interior thoughts in short, abrupt sentences.
  - Tether long interior reflections to the physical surroundings: Internal thoughts must not occupy more than two consecutive paragraphs. Use external environmental or social details to guide their reflection, and to reset this limit.
  - Break paragraphs when the interior focus shifts: When a character’s internal perspective or emotional orientation changes, this is a justification for a new paragraph.
  - Land an emotional beat in the final sentence of a paragraph: Accumulate narrative momentum through long, descriptive sentences, then deliver the emotional conclusion in a compressed, simple final sentence.
  - Subject your characters to grueling refinement: Optimism must be earned. Characters must achieve their goals through hard work or difficult choices. 
  - Anchor internal crises to visceral details: Avoid abstract emotional tracking by forcing characters to interact tangibly with the people around them and their environment.
  - Start scenes as close to the end as possible: Plunge into the momentum of a scenario. Logistical introductions, travel framing, and arrival routines are not the focus; quickly explain them if needed (omit if not) and move past them.
  - Bar the final sentence of a scene from interpreting its meaning: The closing line of a chapter or major scene block must not summarize or moralize the emotional outcome directly.

# STYLE GUIDE & DIALOGUE BIBLE

## 1. NARRATIVE REGISTER & POV
- **CHOSEN_POV:** Dual Third-Person Limited Architecture.
  - *Act I to Midpoint (Chapters 1–9):* Anchored strictly to **Professor Arthur Pendleton (C-05)**. Captures his erudition, aristocratic charm, and improvisational maneuvering. Operates through an **unreliable narrator framework inspired by *The White Tiger* and *The Usual Suspects***: Arthur's internal monologue is strictly truthful, but subtly manipulates perspective. He conceals his creation of the horned chimera by framing his obsessive anxiety around **keeping the head private** and protecting its delicate, bespoke crown from pedantic catalogers and crude hands. He never tells a factual falsehood in thought or deduction, but recontextualizes his panic as aristocratic connoisseurship and scholarly stewardship.
  - *Midpoint to Resolution (Chapters 10–16):* When the chimera is unmasked under lanterns in Carriage D, the narrative perspective pivots to **Pepi Wagner (C-04)**. Captures Pepi's sharp, tactile working-class observations: examining floorboards, finding the unseated hinge pin and dropped retaining collar, noting the microscopic tool wear flaw and luggage logistics, and watching Arthur's suave performance from the outside.
- **PRIMARY_POV_CHARACTERS:** Professor Arthur Pendleton (C-05, Chapters 1–9) and Pepi Wagner (C-04, Chapters 10–16), partnered in inquiry and challenged by junior archaeologist Hector Periwinkle (C-07).
- **TONAL_TARGET:** Crisp, witty, fast-paced 1930s puzzle mystery at **Level 3.5 on the Seriousness Scale**. Touchstones: *Clue* (ensemble friction), *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (stylized staging), and *Knives Out* (class satire and physical deduction) enriched by the theatrical audacity and charm of Maurice Leblanc's *Arsène Lupin*.
- **TONAL_GUARDRAILS (AVOID OVERCORRECTION):**
  - *Guardrail Against Silly Farce:* Characters are articulate, proud professionals under pressure; they never turn into cartoon clowns or slapstick buffoons. Humor comes from wounded vanity, strict manners, and dramatic irony.
  - *Guardrail Against Morose Melodrama:* Stakes are urgent and ruinous (academic fraud, ruined careers, factory collapse, and social disgrace), but the story bars graphic blood, butchery, and bleak nihilism.
- **PACING_PRINCIPLE:** Fast-moving, scene-driven. Every scene opens on dialogue or physical action, keeps a brisk pace, and closes on a hook, newly surfaced contradiction, or active complication.

## 2. PROSE EXECUTION RULES

1. **Rule 1 (The Concrete Vintage Detail — The Three-Sentence Law):** Ground every scene in a specific 1930s physical object, texture, or sound within the first three sentences. Prefer specific objects (walnut walls, steaming tea, track clatter, coal soot) over vague mood-setting. Pick a single, exact descriptor over stacked adjectives (e.g. "a chaise" rather than "an ornate, sprawling, velvet chair").
2. **Rule 2 (The Subtext & Deflection Law):** Dialogue never states what is obvious from action; suspects never speak in dry exposition. Every line of dialogue is an evasion, a deflection, a polite status insult, or an anxious defense of a secret. Characters never narrate what they are currently doing or holding.
3. **Rule 3 (Comedic Whimsy with Real Stakes):** Humor arises from character vanity, class friction, and social awkwardness under pressure. Wit must enhance, never diminish, the genuine personal and professional perils confronting the cast.
4. **Rule 4 (Sentence Rhythm):** Modulate sentence lengths dynamically. Use punchy 6–14 word sentences for physical discovery, tension spikes, and confrontation. Use balanced 18–30 word sentences for deductive deduction. Avoid poetic rhythm and long, low-energy passages; prose moves with the speed of an express train.
5. **Rule 5 (Fair-Play Plain Sight & The Off-Page Crime Window):** The reader possesses every physical clue, timeline anomaly, and behavioral tell observed by the active POV character. The crime during the Landquart Tunnel is strictly off-page; the reader experiences the blackout alongside the passengers in the salon and corridors. Critical evidence (unseated hinge pin, dropped brass collar, soot shadow, bone cement, microscopic tool wear) must be described cleanly in plain sight. Locked-room mechanisms must rely on deliberate physical methods rather than accidental jams or dropped business cards.
6. **Rule 6 (Focus on Reader Experience — SOP 6):** When an event, clue discovery, or dramatic turn occurs, do not explain its significance—let events and actions speak for themselves. Prioritize how and why a human reader will enjoy the scene over in-universe lore or technical pedantry.
7. **Rule 7 (Avoid Overcorrection & Mannerism Budget — SOP 7):** Meter recurring character mannerisms to arise naturally from situational anxiety and psychological pressure: each character's signature tell appears **2 to 4 times across the novel**, and **never more than once per scene**. Mannerisms highlight moments of acute stress rather than becoming repetitive, automated habits.
8. **Rule 8 (Anti-Macabre & Anti-Steampunk Boundaries):** Maintain classic puzzle standards. Zero gore, zero corpses, and zero graphic viscera. Prohibit steampunk jargon (aether, steam-pistons, clockwork automata, brass cogs). Puzzles rely on standard 1930s technology, simple locks, and mechanical tools.
9. **Rule 9 (In Media Res & Active Hooks):** No warm-up sentences or scenic filler. The first sentence of every chapter and scene plunges into action or dialogue. Every scene and chapter ends on an active hook, unanswered question, or contradiction.
10. **Rule 10 (Syntactic Economy & Punctuation Rigor):** Avoid semicolons and em-dashes in narration to maintain clean, driving sentences. Keep comma density low. Em-dashes are permitted strictly within dialogue to represent interruptions or cutoffs. Cap adjective density at one or two per noun. Strip weak qualifiers ("seemed," "almost," "perhaps").
11. **Rule 11 (Arthur's Truthful Manipulation & Concealment Law):** In Chapters 1–9, Arthur's narration conceals his creation of the horned chimera without ever lying to the reader. His internal monologue must actively allude to keeping the head private, avoiding crude inspection, and guarding its delicate cranial crown from dogmatic pedants (Hector and Elsbeth). He leads the inquiry not to play the hero, but to ensure that he recovers the head before anyone else can examine the crate under lanterns. Every deduction he makes is factually accurate, subtly deflecting scrutiny toward other suspects while keeping his own private creation secure.
